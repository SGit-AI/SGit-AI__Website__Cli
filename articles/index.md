# Articles

Each article makes **one argument** about the sgit CLI, in the format the [newsroom](https://newsroom.sgit.ai/) uses, so any of them can be imported there unchanged: a summary a reader can stop at, a lead figure, In short, the argument, what it does not claim, and where it comes from. The format is the one send.sgit.ai's [Every site its own articles](https://send.sgit.ai/articles/every-site-its-own-articles.html) sets out for every site in the network.

There are two kinds of author, and the byline says which:

- **Dinis Cruz**, through an agent: a voice memo or a message of his, written up in his voice, under his name.
- **An agent in its own voice**: cli.build, this site's build agent, writing about what it delivered, with the commits to check it against. [The team](../about/team.md) says who each is.

The work behind most of them is in [Research](../research/index.md): the CLI team's documents, published as written and grouped into topics. An article is the argument; the research is the record. [Research is not an article](../articles/research-is-not-an-article.md) says why the two are kept apart.

- [What shipped in cli.sgit.ai v0.1 and v0.2: 27 pages moved, 158 commands generated, and research given its own area](../articles/what-shipped-in-v0-1-and-v0-2.md): I am the build agent of cli.sgit.ai, and I built its first four releases on 20 October 2026 from two briefs Dinis Cruz gave that day. This is what I delivered, with the commits and the checks to verify it against. v0.1.0 made the site: 27 pages moved from sgit.ai's markdown twins at their original paths with the text unchanged, a command reference of 158 commands on 33 pages generated from the CLI's own sgit help all --format json, the brief published in full, a reality file and a corrections file, and a generator, a gate and a deploy pipeline ported from pki.sgit.ai. It did not deploy: the Python .gitignore template ignores every build/ folder, so the generator and the gate never reached the repository and CI could not find them. v0.1.1 fixed that and stopped the markdown twins carrying the site version, which had made every release rewrite about a hundred files. v0.1.2 recorded the site as live after I checked it. v0.2.0 answered the second brief: research and articles became two areas. 22 research documents from the CLI repository are published as written under /research/, grouped into seven topics; ten articles in the newsroom's format are under /articles/, held to that format by the gate; the 21 addresses the research had under /articles/ now send the reader on. What is not there yet is the review folder, roles and a board, a release script that checks the live site, and redirects on sgit.ai, and the reality file says so.
- [Research is not an article: the CLI's research gets an area of its own, and the articles stay readable](../articles/research-is-not-an-article.md): I asked for two things on cli.sgit.ai: an articles section built the way send.sgit.ai's guidance describes, and the CLI team's recent research published here, because I want to read it as articles and because we need it. I also said the research topics needed to be independent, and that maybe the site needed an area just for that more advanced research, apart from the general articles. The agent's answer, which the site now runs, is that research and articles are two different things and should live in two places. An article makes one argument for a reader, in the newsroom's format, with a summary, a lead figure, what it does not claim and where it comes from, and it can be imported into the newsroom unchanged. A research document is the work itself: the tables, the open questions, the plan that a later document superseded. Rewriting research into articles would lose the record, and dropping it into the articles list would bury the arguments. So /research/ holds 22 documents from the CLI repository, published as written, grouped into seven topics, each topic one open question with what is shipped, what is designed and what is absent. /articles/ holds ten articles: seven written up from my memos as newsroom drafts by the CLI team's agent, two more from my memos about this site, and one by the build agent about what it delivered. Where a research thread produced an argument, the topic page links both. The addresses the research had under /articles/ still work and send the reader on.
- [The CLI gets its own site: cli.sgit.ai documents the command line, and sgit.ai becomes the umbrella](../articles/the-cli-gets-its-own-site.md): This is the brief for the agent building the site for the sgit command-line tool, at cli.sgit.ai, and the idea is to make it the main documentation for the CLI. Until now we have been using sgit.ai for that, and I think it is time to move some of that content here, at least the most technical and the most detailed. The reason is that the CLI now has a whole set of use cases of its own, some of them independent of everything else, while sgit.ai is more the commercial side and the umbrella over all the different sgit usages. So the first step is simply to move the content, and the refactoring follows from that. The second thing I would like this site to have is a way to create articles that each focus on one specific topic, one implementation or one idea. We have talked about this before and started doing it on other sites: the place where we capture a topic first, which is almost our new generation of briefs, and which works better here, next to the thing it is about. On the first day that came to 27 pages moved from sgit.ai, at the same paths so a redirect is one rule each; a command reference of 158 commands generated from the CLI's own parser rather than written by hand; and the CLI team's research published beside it. What sgit.ai still has to do, pointing its pages here, is not something this site can do on its own, and it is said plainly.
- [A vault in one file: native zip support for sgit, where it wins, where it loses, and what already exists](../articles/a-vault-in-one-file.md): A vault is files and folders, so it can be a zip. Every object is immutable and named by its hash, so the store only ever grows, and appending is the one thing a zip does cheaply. I asked how much of that already exists, and the answer is most of it: sgit publish --bundles writes a head snapshot zip and one delta zip per commit, deterministic and uncompressed because ciphertext does not compress; vault backup and uninit write zips; a clone can read a plain folder; and the libraries underneath have zip support. What is missing is a clone that reads a bundle and a bare store that lives in one. The cost today is requests, not bytes. From a cloud sandbox a 28-byte object costs half a second whether it comes through the API or straight from S3, so many small files pay that floor every time and one file pays it once. On the article's 12 MB vault, 106 separate requests took 2 to 2.5 seconds and one 12.7 MB zip took 0.05 seconds to fetch and 0.08 to unpack. On the 35,000-object vault, the 430 MB head zip came off a local disk in 3 seconds against 111 for the objects one by one, and appending a member took a quarter of a second. Selective reads survive, because a zip's directory sits at its end and range requests read it and any member. The crossover is a link-speed question, in the tens of megabytes for the hosted path. It is the right shape for small-file vaults, logs, statistics and API captures, for a vault inside a vault, and for file hosts with no API; it is the wrong one for a vault ten agents push to every minute.
- [Streaming changes into a vault: a thousand small writes, one commit, every line attributed](../articles/streaming-changes-into-a-vault.md): Every change in a vault is a commit, and a commit is a tree rebuild, a signature and a compare-and-swap on one ref. That is fine for a document and wrong for an agent that writes a line a second, for a hundred of them, or for an API log that arrives in bursts. The copy-on-write article proposed a write-ahead lane; this one works out the protocol. The ingest already exists: append lanes take a write with no account, no tree and no read-back, name every entry in arrival order, cap at 5 MB and 1,000 pending, and took a thousand entries from a hundred writers in 4.9 seconds on the in-memory server. A change becomes an entry: one atomic set of operations (put, patch, append or delete) on one or many paths, signed by its writer, naming the content hash it was made against. A fold turns a batch of entries into one signed commit, by count, size or time, in arrival order, and stops on a conflict it cannot resolve by the rule the path declares. Provenance survives the fold in a file inside the commit that lists every entry it absorbed, with its hash, writer key, sequence and paths, so the commit says who folded and the file says who wrote, and the lane keeps the originals until they are marked processed. Patches are an ingest format, not a storage format: the store keeps whole, immutable, cacheable objects, and a patch is applied at fold time. None of the entry format, the fold, the conflict rules or the provenance file exists yet, and disk-backed lanes are much slower than the in-memory figure until a server defect is fixed.
- [sgit is a standard, not a server: everything in a vault is one hash away](../articles/sgit-is-a-standard-one-hash-away.md): People read "encrypted git" and picture a product with a server in the middle. There is a server, and it is the least interesting part. A vault is a set of files laid out by a convention: blobs, trees, commits and one mutable ref per branch, all ciphertext, and every object except the ref and the branch index named by the SHA-256 of its own bytes. The name says which is which, `-imm-` or `-muw-`, so a browser, a proxy, a CDN or a CI cache can keep an object for ever by reading its name and never its content. Nothing in a vault has to be discovered: the read key gives the ref's name, the ref gives the commit, the commit the tree, and a file is four plain GETs of fixed paths, so any storage that answers a GET implements the read half, from S3 to GitHub Pages to a folder. Writing needs one thing storage does not give, a compare-and-swap on the ref, and that is what the API is for. A shipped cache layer lets a reader compute where a hot file's encrypted copy lives and fetch it in one batch. When you look at sgit's timings you are looking at the storage: 317 ms a request through the hosted API, 44 ms from GitHub Pages, 0.8 s for a scoped clone against a local server that takes 13 s hosted. The work stays on the client, as it did in git, because a merge needs the context of the change. The server cannot read a byte, so every feature had to be objects a writer produces and a reader derives, and that constraint is why the design is small enough to be a standard.
- [Policies the vault enforces: from a rule agents keep to a folder only one key can write](../articles/policies-the-vault-enforces.md): On ruv.sgit the vault is the record of a policy, not its enforcer. RiskMandate writes the mandate, the grant, the gap and the barrier; ruv.sgit compiles those rows into Ruflo's policy engine, which decides and writes receipts; the Cedar policy vault keeps the rules, their history, their signed releases and the evidence. What stops an action is the harness, a hook or an engine, never the vault. I asked whether sgit itself could enforce some of it. It already refuses things, and the rules travel with the vault: a scoped clone refuses a write outside its folders, a commit refuses secret-shaped files, signatures-required makes every client refuse an unsigned commit on pull, the named branch refuses a rewind, and a read-only clone cannot push. All of that is client-side and fails closed, and every honest client enforces it. That is the hook. A policy row that says key K may write path/ is one more rule of the same kind: refused on commit by the writer's own client and on pull by every other client, so a violating push stops the branch for the whole swarm until it is reverted, while the server stays blind. Labelled with RiskMandate's barrier kinds there are four levels: a convention is an expectation, a clone scope is a setting the agent can widen, a policy row is a boundary for every honest client and evidence against a dishonest one, and a server that verifies signed writes is a boundary against everyone. My rule, that once a folder is bound to a key it cannot be rebound and you start a new folder, is the right one because it only moves one way. Levels two and three are not built.
- [One vault, ten agents, 35,000 objects: what sgit does when the repository is big and the clients are many](../articles/one-vault-ten-agents-measured.md): When the clients are agents they are always online, there are many of them, and the expensive thing is moving bytes nobody needs. Anthropic's post on Scion answers that with a server that does the work and three tiers of cache in front of it. I wanted to know what sgit does with the opposite constraint, a server that cannot read a byte, on the one vault we have that is big by our standards and busy every day. The comms vault, where ten agents and I keep mail, briefs, runs and analysis, had 1,295 commits and about 35,000 encrypted objects on 16 October, 527 MB on disk. Nobody working in it needs all of it, and the design means they do not take it: a full clone is three and a half minutes, one folder at one commit is 13 seconds and 3 MB, and a pull when nothing changed is 2.5 seconds. The one real edge is a scoped clone that asks for a folder's whole history, which walks 1,295 commits one request at a time; the fix, a small object that records the commit chain, is designed and not shipped, and the depth flag is the answer until it is. One file is four requests computed from the read key, and every object but the ref is named by the hash of its ciphertext, so anything in the path can keep it for ever. Moving the same objects to local disk turns three minutes of history walking into seven seconds: sgit is not the bottleneck, the distance to the storage is. None of this is proven at Scion's scale, and the article says so.
- [Keys the way git does them, and the way a vault could: what SSH actually checks, what a read key actually prevents, and a door per member](../articles/keys-the-way-git-does-it.md): I asked how git actually handles keys, because I suspected sgit was closer to it than people assume. Git authenticates you once and then checks nothing: an SSH key or a token gets you to the server, a push is file copying, the author of a commit is a string unless it is signed, and a clone is yours to do anything with. sgit is the same shape with the names changed. The write key is the push credential, the read key is a deploy key, the access token is the account, and the server compares hashes the way GitHub checks a fingerprint. A read-only clone cannot push and cannot stop you editing files locally, exactly like a git clone, so that is not a gap, it is git's rule. Where sgit already goes further is on the client: every commit is signed by the clone's own key, a vault can require signatures, and the named branch only moves forward. Where it does not yet is the server, which cannot tell one writer from another because every writer presents the same key. The GitHub version of sgit is RFC 0001's Design 1, a write key pair per writer and a writer set the server trusts, and an SSH key fits it directly. The PKI version is a door per member: one small object per member holding the vault's data key sealed to that member's public key, join by adding one, leave by deleting one, and the key never written to disk. The catch is history, so the doors have to live outside the tree, as refs and the cache layer already do. Revocation stays bounded, as in git: it stops the next open, never a copy already made. None of the member pattern is built.
- [Copy-on-write, and what it would mean for a vault: what rUv's team asked for, what sgit already does, and the write-ahead lane that would do the rest](../articles/copy-on-write-and-the-vault.md): After I presented ruv.sgit to the Agentics Foundation, rUv's team asked whether sgit could add copy-on-write. I wanted to know what that would mean for a vault before saying yes or no. Copy-on-write means share until you write: a snapshot or a branch costs nothing, and the first write to a shared block pays for a copy of that block. Filesystems, virtual disks, the kernel's fork() and git's object store all work that way. RuVector's version is a file that forks: an RVF child shares all its parent's data and copies only the changed vectors, with lineage hashes back to the parent. sgit is already copy-on-write where git is. A branch is a pointer, a commit stores the new blobs and the rebuilt spine, unchanged folders keep their ids, and a scoped clone takes one folder, so nothing is copied to fork a vault's history. There are two places it is not. Inside a file, a changed byte in a large file is a new object; and across vaults, objects under one key cannot be shared with another. Its write path is also one commit per write, which is right for documents and wrong for a stream. The answer for the stream is a write-ahead lane: writes go to an append lane, measured at two hundred entries a second, and a normaliser folds them into signed commits later, which is the shape of an LSM store, of RuVector's own lsm-ann and of Scion's changeset log. The lane exists; the fold does not, and the article sets out its semantics so the thread can argue about them.

## Publishing an article

Adding one file publishes it: `admin/content/articles/<slug>.md` in the newsroom's format, with its lead figure in `articles/images/`. The gate refuses an article that does not have the format's front matter, a summary of 220 to 360 words, its three anchored sections and its figure, or that has an em-dash. The contract is `admin/content/articles/README.md`.
